    Module 3 — Material Guide¶

    🖨️ Module 3 ⏱ ~1.5 hrs 🟢 Fundamentals

    Content adapted from the Original Prusa MK4S / MK3.9S Handbook §7 and the Original Prusa i3 MK3S Handbook §12. © Prusa Research s.r.o. — used for educational purposes.

    The printer is compatible with almost all filaments available. Individual materials may differ not only in color but also in mechanical and optical properties, or even in printing difficulty.

    Full material table online

    Due to limited space in the handbooks, this guide provides a brief overview of popular materials. Visit help.prusa3d.com/materials for a full overview of the wide range of printing materials, and prusament.com for Prusa's own in-house filament line (every spool is thoroughly tested for strand diameter, color consistency, and mechanical properties — scan the QR code on the spool to inspect its parameters).

    The materials below are sorted from the most to the least commonly used. If you're new to 3D printing, your first filament should definitely be PLA. Only when you are limited by some of PLA's disadvantages, try other filaments like PETG or ASA. The additional materials at the end of this module are very specific to particular applications.

    3.1 PLA¶

    PLA is the most commonly used material for 3D printing. It prints easily and prints from PLA are very hard. The perfect choice for printing large objects due to low shrinkage (prints don't warp on the bed) and for printing detailed small models. On the MK3S, it is the only material well suited for printing 50 micron layers (Ultradetail resolution).

    Advantages Disadvantages
    Easy to print, suitable for beginners Brittle and inflexible
    Easily print small detailed models Low temperature resistance (50–60 °C)
    Trouble-free printing of larger objects Difficult post-processing
    Almost odorless Not suitable for outdoor use (low UV and temperature resistance)
    Affordable, wide range of colors

    Typical uses: prototypes, toys, action figures, jewelry and small detailed models in general, architecture models and more.

    Tips and tricks: Prints best on a smooth (or satin) print sheet. When post-processing PLA prints, it is best to use wet sanding — if you use sandpaper dry, the heat generated by friction can start to deform the printed object. PLA can only be dissolved in chemicals such as chloroform or heated benzene. For gluing, a good quality superglue is sufficient; certain types of PLA can also be glued with acetone.

    • Nozzle temperature: 215 °C
    • Heated print bed: 50–60 °C
    • Print surface: make sure the print sheet is clean, as per the Regular Maintenance chapter
    • Nozzle temperature: 215 °C
    • Bed temperature: 50–60 °C
    • Heatbed: keep the surface clean; PLA adheres well to the smooth PEI sheet

    3.2 PETG¶

    PETG is one of the most popular materials for 3D printing. It is a great choice for parts that will be subject to mechanical stress. Compared to PLA, it has a higher temperature resistance, it is more flexible and less brittle. Thanks to its low thermal expansion, it holds well to the bed and does not warp. Printing with it is almost as easy as with PLA, but unlike PLA it offers much better mechanical properties. Parts of Prusa's own printers are printed with PETG!

    Advantages Disadvantages
    High temperature resistance Not suitable for printing small/highly detailed models
    Easy printing Nozzle can leave thin filaments (stringing)
    Low thermal expansion Problematic bridging and overhangs
    Durable and tough Strong adhesion to the bed
    Easy machining (sanding) Cannot be smoothed with commonly available solvents
    Printing almost without smell Removal of supports can be difficult
    Glossy surface, good adhesion of layers

    Typical use: mechanical parts, holders and cases, waterproof prints (flower pots).

    Tips and tricks: PETG requires a higher heatbed temperature (85 °C). PETG usually has worse results when bridging two points, plus PETG tends to string — it leaves fine plastic strings on the surface of the print (which can be relatively easily removed). Stringing can be reduced by setting appropriate retraction and using lower printing temperatures — stick to the values in the PrusaSlicer profiles. The print must be well cooled. However, if you want the most durable print, try turning off the print fan — a higher temperature will cause the layers to stick better to each other. Generally, print the first few layers with the fan off (for adhesion), then turn it on at 50 % power.

    • Nozzle temperature: 240 °C
    • Bed temperature: 70–90 °C
    • Print surface: a textured or satin print sheet needs no special preparation, just keep it clean and free of grease. If you use a smooth PEI sheet, apply a thin layer of glue stick — PETG may adhere too strongly to the sheet surface, making prints difficult to remove.
    • Nozzle temperature: 240 °C
    • Bed temperature: 80–100 °C
    • Heatbed: you shouldn't use isopropyl alcohol to clean the bed — the adhesion may be too strong. If you have nothing else on hand, use the bundled glue as a separator after cleaning. Windex (or similar window cleaner) is a great option for cleaning the heatbed for PET.

    3.3 ASA (ABS)¶

    ASA and ABS are very similar materials — in many respects ASA is better. ASA is UV-stable compared to ABS and shrinks slightly less during printing. When it comes to post-processing, ABS and ASA can be similar, but ASA is currently more popular, so we focus mainly on it.

    ASA is a strong and versatile material. A higher melting temperature than PLA gives ASA good thermal resistance — prints show no signs of deformation up to around 100 °C. Unfortunately, compared to PLA, ASA has a very high thermal expansion, which complicates printing, especially larger models. Even with a heatbed set to 100 °C, the print can start to warp and detach from the bed. ASA also produces a noticeable odor during printing.

    Advantages Disadvantages
    High impact and wear resistance (lower than PETG) Difficult printing
    Very good thermal resistance Tendency to warp (printing in an enclosed box is recommended)
    Suitable for outdoor use — UV stable Unpleasant smell when printing (contains styrene)
    Soluble in acetone — can be used for gluing
    Can be smoothed with acetone vapors
    Detailed prints without stringing
    Easy post-processing (sanding, cutting, etc.)

    Typical uses: cases and protective covers, prototypes, spare parts, toys and figures, parts suitable for exterior use.

    Tips and tricks: Printing with ASA/ABS is much easier if the printer is placed inside an environment with increased stable temperatures (an enclosure). This significantly reduces both deformation and layer separation. Thanks to acetone, it is easy to join several prints together — lightly rub the contact surfaces and press the parts together. It is also possible to smooth prints with acetone vapors and get a perfectly glossy surface. Be careful when handling acetone!

    • Printing temperature: 220–275 °C
    • Bed temperature: 90–110 °C (larger objects require higher temperature)
    • Print surface: works best with a satin print sheet (no special preparation, just keep it clean). On a textured/smooth sheet, apply a glue stick.
    • Nozzle temperature: 245–265 °C
    • Bed temperature: 90–110 °C (larger objects require higher temperature)
    • Heatbed: keep the surface clean as described in the sheet preparation chapter. The printer should be enclosed.

    3.4 PC (polycarbonate) and PC Blend¶

    Polycarbonate (PC) is a technical material boasting excellent strength, tensile strength, and resistance to high temperatures. It is quite demanding to print, making it suitable mainly for advanced users — this does not apply to Prusament PC Blend, which is much easier to print compared to other polycarbonates. Polycarbonate surpasses all of the aforementioned materials in its mechanical, chemical, and thermal resistance.

    Advantages Disadvantages
    High-temperature resistance Pure polycarbonate is highly hygroscopic
    High strength and tension resistance High nozzle and bed temperatures
    Clear polycarbonate is transparent Strong warping, especially for large models
    Good electrical insulation properties Mild smell during printing
    Separating layer application recommended
    High price

    Typical uses: technical components for which we require higher resistance to mechanical wear and tear and high temperatures.

    Tips and tricks: consider printing in a closed box to prevent warping; enable the Brim feature in PrusaSlicer — set it higher than the default outline, ideally to the whole height of the print; add a Skirt around small objects; do not print in low-temperature zones.

    • Nozzle temperature: 270–275 °C
    • Bed temperature: 110 °C for the first layer, 115 °C for the following layers
    • Print surface: textured and smooth sheets with a layer of stick glue offer the best adhesion. Apply a separating layer of glue to prevent wear/damage to the surface.

    3.5 PVB¶

    Polyvinyl butyral (PVB) is a material that can be easily smoothed with isopropyl alcohol (IPA). Prints, when properly set up, are clear and transparent, making PVB a suitable material for printing vases, lamp shades, and other decorative models. The printing settings are similar to those for PLA, but the mechanical properties of PVB are slightly better.

    Advantages Disadvantages
    Similar printing settings to PLA Lower adhesion between layers
    Transparent filament Hygroscopic material (absorbs moisture)
    Suitable for decorative models Higher price
    Chemical smoothing with IPA
    Good toughness and tensile strength (similar to PETG and PLA)
    Less prone to warping (less than PLA)

    Typical uses: transparent (translucent) models — jewelry, vases, lamp shades, etc.

    Tips and tricks: PVB has good adhesion to a clean smooth or satin print sheet, while textured print sheets have rather poor adhesion. For translucent prints you will later smooth with IPA, use a bigger nozzle (0.8 mm) and enable "Spiral vase" mode in PrusaSlicer. Models can be smoothed in IPA vapors, by immersion in an IPA bath, or by directly applying IPA to the surface (sprayer or brush). Store the filament dry — PVB absorbs moisture, which can negatively affect print quality. Always return it to its plastic bag with silica gel, or dry it for 4 hours at 60 °C before printing.

    • Nozzle temperature: 215±10 °C
    • Bed temperature: 75 °C
    • Print surface: do not use the standard textured sheet; PVB adheres better to a clean smooth or satin sheet.

    3.6 Flexible materials¶

    Flexible filaments are typically very strong and elastic materials. Whether you are printing a phone case, a housing for an action camera, or even wheels for an RC car, it is better to use a flexible material. These materials are often expensive, not very common, and not suitable for beginners.

    Before you start printing with Flex, clean the nozzle from the previous material by inserting PLA into the preheated extruder and pushing out all the previous material. For Prusa printers, Semiflex or Flexfill 98A, or Filatech FilaFlex40 are recommended (tuned profiles exist in PrusaSlicer).

    Advantages Disadvantages
    Flexibility and elasticity Requires a special procedure for inserting filament
    Minimal shrinkage Very poor bridging and overhangs
    Excellent adhesion of layers Requires lower print speed
    Great resistance to wear Higher price
    Absorbs moisture — must be stored in a dry environment
    • Nozzle temperature: 220–260 °C
    • Bed temperature: 40–85 °C (larger objects require higher temperature)
    • Print surface: on a smooth or satin sheet, apply a separation layer (glue stick is ideal). Textured sheets with a powder-coated PEI surface need no separation layer — the print holds well and detaches easily after cooling.
    • Nozzle temperature: 230–260 °C
    • Bed temperature: 45–65 °C
    • Heatbed: some very soft materials can adhere very strongly to the smooth PEI sheet and require a glue stick separator. Not necessary with the textured powder-coated PEI. When inserting Flex, loosen the pressure on the idler as much as possible.

    3.7 PA (polyamide) / PA11CF¶

    Polyamide (also known as Nylon) is a versatile material known for its durability. It is commonly used for 3D printing special models due to its high difficulty in printing (not applicable to PA11CF) and higher costs. There are several types of polyamide, which differ in properties such as temperature resistance, water absorption and adhesion. Prusament PA11CF has great temperature resistance (up to 192 °C), strong chemical resistance, and prints easily. Some polyamides, including PA11CF, are reinforced with carbon fibers to reduce shrinkage, often at the expense of mechanical strength. PA11CF is recommended for extremely stressed parts, such as plastic engine components.

    Advantages Disadvantages
    Great temperature resistance (up to 192 °C) Not suitable for small/highly detailed models
    Resistance to a range of chemicals Prone to warping (not applicable to PA11CF)
    Hard and resilient in thick layers, flexible in thin layers Challenging bridging and overhangs
    Smooth glossy surface of clean PA Strong (or too weak) adhesion to the surface
    Excellent layer adhesion Cannot be smoothed with common solvents
    Suitable insulation material Difficult to remove supports
    Highly hygroscopic material

    Typical use: mechanical parts, holders and housings, electrical insulation parts, movable parts, and parts requiring high temperature resistance.

    Tips and tricks: it is absolutely essential to keep the filament dry — otherwise adhesion and overall quality significantly worsen. Dry the filament for at least 4 hours at a maximum of 90 °C before printing. Prints from dried polyamide have a smooth, glossy surface, while carbon-fiber-reinforced materials have a matte surface. When printing polyamides, use an enclosed printer with active filtration or keep the printer in a well-ventilated room — potentially hazardous particles are released when printing (all) PA, and the higher ambient temperature also reduces warping and improves layer adhesion.

    • Nozzle temperature: 240–285 °C
    • Bed temperature: 70–115 °C
    • Print surface: use the special PA Nylon print sheet for most polyamides — it ensures ideal adhesion even when cleaned only with water. Do not print polyamides on a smooth sheet; on a textured or satin sheet apply a layer of paper glue (glue stick — Kores / PVA).

    3.8 Additional materials (MK3S handbook)¶

    The older handbook documents several further materials. These are condensed for reference:

    Material What it is Nozzle Bed Notes
    CPE (copolyester) Similar to PETG, with mechanical properties that surpass standard PETG 255–275 °C 70–90 °C Chemically resistant, excellent layer adhesion; not soluble in acetone; store dry
    Nylon (Taulman Bridge) Very strong, ductile, versatile — mechanical components 240 °C 80–90 °C Must be stored dry; glue stick on smooth PEI
    XT (ColorFabb) Tough and ductile, good temperature resistance, glossy finish 240–260 °C 60–90 °C Food safe; slightly worse bridging
    HT (ColorFabb) High-temperature copolyester (up to 100 °C) 250–280 °C 100–110 °C Higher price; worse adhesion to the bed
    Composites (wood, metal, carbon…) Plastic base + dust of a second material 190–210 °C 50–70 °C Abrasive — use a hardened nozzle; wood composites like ≥0.5 mm nozzles
    HIPS Support material for ASA/ABS; dissolves in limonene 215–225 °C 90–110 °C Leaves smooth prints with no marks after support removal
    PVA / BVOH Water-soluble support material for PLA 185–210 °C 40–60 °C Store in a dry bag with silica gel — highly hygroscopic
    nGen Similar to PETG but styrene-free 240 °C 80–100 °C Smooth surface; slight warping
    PC-ABS (E3D) Improved ABS: stronger, stiffer, more heat resistant 270–280 °C 100–110 °C Good bridging; warps
    PP (polypropylene) Partially elastic, chemical- and temperature-resistant 210–230 °C 95–100 °C Bad adhesion — wide transparent adhesive tape on the surface works best
    T-Glase (Taulman) High-strength; temperature resistant to 78 °C 230–240 °C 90–110 °C Great layer adhesion; minimal warping

    Creating profiles for new materials¶

    Each manufacturer delivers a slightly different material — even two brands of PLA can behave differently, and different colors of the same material may vary. For best results, experiment with nozzle and heatbed temperature, fan speed, print speed, and material flow — all of which can be adjusted while printing in the Tune menu on the LCD panel.

    For materials without a print profile: take the recommended settings from the manufacturer, find the nearest material profile in PrusaSlicer, edit it and save it as new. Proceed with test prints and adjust the settings one after another in the Tune menu. Once happy with the print quality, save the settings to your PrusaSlicer profile.

    Reset the Tune menu before the next print

    Be sure to reset the values in the Tune menu before the next print — they persist until you change them again or reset the printer.

    Material selection quick reference¶

    Need Reach for
    First print, general prototyping, toys PLA
    Mechanical parts, cases, watertight prints PETG
    Outdoor / UV exposure, heat resistance ASA / ABS
    Engineering parts, high heat PC / PC Blend, PA
    Decorative, glass-like smoothing PVB
    Grip, cases, RC parts Flexible
    Dissolvable supports HIPS, PVA/BVOH
